Net Force and Free-Body Diagrams

What is net force?

A force is a push or a pull that acts on an object. Forces are measured in newtons (N).

Most of the time, more than one force acts on an object at once. The net force is the overall force: the single force that could replace all the others and have the same effect. Three facts tell you how to find it:

Forces acting in the same direction add together to determine the net force.
Forces acting in opposite directions subtract to determine the net force.
The larger force points in the direction of the net force.

Try it: Net Force Explorer

Change the size and direction of the two forces on the box. Watch the purple net force arrow and the math underneath.

Free-body diagrams

A free-body diagram (FBD) shows every force acting on one object. On your fact sheet, a free-body diagram includes:

  1. A labeled arrow representing each force. Each arrow begins on the object and points in the direction in which the force acts.
  2. A list of the forces, indicating the object applying the force and the object experiencing the force.

Make the arrow for a bigger force longer than the arrow for a smaller force.

Justify Your Answer With a Fact

How we justify answers

On the AP exam, a correct answer with no explanation earns little or no credit. In this class, every justification has two parts:

Fact: A fact of physics, stated almost word-for-word from your fact sheet. Always start here.
Reasoning: Apply that fact to this problem. Say what the fact means for these forces and this object.

Keep it short. "Justify your answer" means about two or three sentences, not a paragraph.

Example from class

QUESTIONA cart experiences a 3 N force to the left and a 2 N force to the right. Which way is the net force on the cart? Justify your answer with a fact.

Fact: Forces acting in opposite directions subtract to determine the net force. The larger force points in the direction of the net force.

Reasoning: The larger force points to the left, so the net force is to the left. Forces acting in opposite directions subtract, so the net force is 3 − 2, which is 1 newton left.

Notice that the Reasoning uses the actual numbers and directions from the problem. It connects the fact to this cart.

Acceleration facts

You already know the net force on this cart is 1 N to the left, so its acceleration is to the left. But what does acceleration actually tell you? Here are the acceleration facts from Unit 1:

Acceleration tells how much an object's speed changes in one second.
When an object speeds up, its acceleration is in the direction of motion.
When an object slows down, its acceleration is opposite the direction of motion.

Acceleration is about how the speed is changing. It is not the same thing as the direction the object is going. Let's test that with a simulation.

Simulation: same forces, different starts

The sim cart has a mass of 1 kg. Set the forces, give the cart a starting push (its starting velocity), and press Play. The dots at the top are a motion diagram: one dot every 0.5 s. The farther apart the dots, the faster the cart is moving.

Record what you saw

Run both trials and record what you saw to unlock the next part.

So which way is the cart moving?

From the forces alone, you can't tell. In both trials the net force was 1 N to the left, but the cart moved left in one trial and right in the other.

Thinking the net force (or acceleration) tells you the direction of motion. Acceleration doesn't say anything about which way something is moving, unless you also know whether it is speeding up or slowing down.

To know which way an object is moving, you need TWO things:

  1. The direction of the acceleration (the same as the direction of the net force), and
  2. Whether the object is speeding up or slowing down.

A net force to the left means the object is either moving left and speeding up, or moving right and slowing down.

Calculating Acceleration: The Two-Step Process

Mass and weight

Before you calculate, you need to know the difference between mass and weight:

Mass tells how much material is contained in an object. The units of mass are kilograms.
Weight is the force of a planet acting on an object (in newtons).
On Earth's surface, the gravitational field g is 10 N/kg. This means that on Earth, 1 kg of mass weighs 10 N.

W = mg, where g on Earth is 10 m/s every second.

Solving problems with unbalanced forces

The two-step problem-solving process

  1. Draw a free-body diagram. (Break angled forces into components, if necessary.)
  2. Write two equations, one for Newton's second law in each direction:
    (up forces) – (down forces) = ma
    (left forces) – (right forces) = ma
You should never get a "negative" net force, so start each equation in the direction of acceleration. For example, if acceleration is downward, write: (down forces) – (up forces) = ma.
Setting a single force equal to ma. Only the net force equals mass times acceleration. Always subtract (or add) all the forces in that direction first.

Scales and Elevators

What does a scale really read?

A normal force is the force of a surface on an object in contact with that surface.
A normal force acts perpendicular to a surface.
A platform scale reads the normal force.

A scale does not read your weight. It reads how hard it pushes up on you (the normal force). The scale reading equals your weight only when the net force on you is zero.

Fact Sheet

Use these almost word-for-word in the "Fact" part of a justification.

Force and Net Force

  • A force is a push or a pull that acts on an object.
  • The units of force are newtons.
  • Forces acting in the same direction add together to determine the net force.
  • Forces acting in opposite directions subtract to determine the net force.
  • The larger force points in the direction of the net force.
  • The net force is in the direction in which forces are unbalanced.
  • The net force is in the direction of acceleration.
  • For all forces other than long-range forces (ex: force of the Earth), objects must be in contact in order to experience a force.

Acceleration

  • Acceleration tells how much an object's speed changes in one second.
  • When an object speeds up, its acceleration is in the direction of motion.
  • When an object slows down, its acceleration is opposite the direction of motion.
  • The units of acceleration are m/s per second.

Equilibrium

  • An object is in equilibrium if it is moving in a straight line at constant speed, or if it remains at rest.
  • The net force on an object in equilibrium is zero (the forces are balanced).

Mass and Weight

  • Mass tells how much material is contained in an object. The units of mass are kilograms.
  • Weight is the force of a planet acting on an object (in newtons).
  • On Earth's surface, the gravitational field g is 10 N/kg. This means that on Earth, 1 kg of mass weighs 10 N.

Normal Force

  • A normal force is the force of a surface on an object in contact with that surface.
  • A normal force acts perpendicular to a surface.
  • A platform scale reads the normal force.

Free-Body Diagrams

  • A labeled arrow representing each force. Each arrow begins on the object and points in the direction in which the force acts.
  • A list of the forces, indicating the object applying the force and the object experiencing the force.

Solving Problems With Unbalanced Forces

  1. Draw a free-body diagram. Break angled forces into components, if necessary.
  2. Write two equations, one for Newton's second law in each direction:
    (up forces) – (down forces) = ma

Never get a "negative" net force: start each equation in the direction of acceleration.
